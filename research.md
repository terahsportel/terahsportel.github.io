# Research

LLADR grew out of doctoral fieldwork in the coconut economy of Keralam, India, during 2007-08, and renewed research there since 2023. Coconut was the entry point: a single commodity that connects agriculture, migration, labour relations, processing, trade, and household consumption. We now carry the same questions into toddy, handloom, and other rural industries.

Our long-term objective is to establish a nuanced understanding of key development transitions influencing agrarian livelihood change, and rural labour dynamics in South India, while creating mechanisms for tangible contributions within communities, including policy briefs and training with industry and government bodies. Correspondence about any of this work, including from students and collaborators working on similar questions, is welcome.

## Current projects

- *Comparative livelihood change in Keralam's coconut economy, 2007/08 to 2023/25.* This project returns to two contrasting regions to examine how livelihoods associated with the industry have changed across a sixteen-year period. A manuscript from this work is under review.
- *Labour and livelihoods in Keralam's toddy sector, 2024 to present.* This project examines labour dynamics, caste-demarcated work, and social mobility within a state-regulated industry worked by largely unorganised labour, drawing on interviews with palm farmers, palm and coconut tappers, toddy shop owners and excise officials.
- *Handloom livelihoods in Keralam, 2026.* This project examines the contraction of a long-protected industry through the accounts of weavers, merchants and co-operative societies. A manuscript from this work is under review.

## Publications

Blackman T, Sportel TS, Walton-Roberts M. 2022. Placing Care Work in the Future of Work Discourse. Glocalism: Journal of Culture, Politics and Innovation, 3. doi: [10.12893/gjcpi.2022.3.6](https://doi.org/10.12893/gjcpi.2022.3.6).

Debates about the future of work are dominated by predictions about automation and job loss. This article argues that they leave out the work that cannot be automated and is growing fastest: care, much of it performed by migrant and racialised women. Bringing together diverse economies, global care chains, and sustainable livelihoods, we set out what a future of work agenda would look like if it took socially just care work seriously.

Sportel TS, Véron R. 2016. Coconut Crisis in Kerala? Mainstream Narrative and Alternative Perspectives. Development and Change, 47: 1051-1077. doi: [10.1111/dech.12260](https://doi.org/10.1111/dech.12260).

Between 2000 and 2002 coconut prices in Kerala collapsed. A dominant narrative claimed cheap imported palm oil had caused the crisis, casting small farmers as its victims. Juxtaposing that narrative with ethnographic research with farmers and traders, we found labour shortages, regional competition and rising costs at work, and smallholders with diversified incomes who did not describe their situation as a crisis. The paper asks why this account proved so durable, and how policy built on it missed the regional differences that shape the coconut economy.

Sportel TS. 2013. Agency within a socially regulated labour market: A study of ‘unorganised’ agricultural labour in Kerala. Geoforum, 47: 42-52. doi: [10.1016/j.geoforum.2013.02.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geoforum.2013.02.007).

Kerala's coconut growers have long reported a shortage of pluckers (harvesting labour). This paper shows the shortage is produced by social relations, not by an absence of workers: caste, class, space, and ethnicity determine who can be hired, at what price and on what terms. Drawing on seventeen months of fieldwork and 569 household surveys, it argues that 'unorganised' workers exercise agency in small, informal ways, and that tools and training programmes cannot resolve a shortage rooted in agrarian relations.
